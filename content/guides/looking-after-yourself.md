When someone dies, the people closest to them are often asked to do two very hard things at once:
grieve, and handle a long list of practical tasks. It's no wonder so many people feel exhausted,
foggy or numb. This guide is about you.

## Grief can feel like many things

There is no right way to grieve. You might feel sad, angry, guilty, relieved, numb or all of these
in a single afternoon. You might cry often or not at all. Many people find that:

- it's hard to concentrate or remember things
- sleep and appetite change
- the body feels tired, heavy or tense
- small tasks feel enormous
- grief comes in waves, sometimes when you least expect it

These are normal reactions to loss. They usually soften with time, though grief may stay with you in
some form for a long while.

## The basics really do matter

When everything feels urgent, the basics are often the first thing to go. Small steps help:

- **Eat something,** even if it's simple. Accept meals from friends.
- **Drink water.**
- **Rest when you can.** If sleep is hard, lying down with your eyes closed still helps.
- **Get outside for a few minutes.** A short walk or some fresh air can ease tension.
- **Take any regular medication.** Grief can make it easy to forget.

## Pace the practical work

Most of the tasks after a death can wait. A few ideas that help many people:

- **Do one or two things a day,** then stop. Tendwell shows you only a few things at a time for this
  reason.
- **Choose your best time of day** for calls and paperwork, and keep the rest of the day lighter.
- **Write things down.** Notes mean you don't have to hold everything in your head.
- **Let things wait.** Big decisions, like selling a home or giving away belongings, can usually wait
  until you feel steadier.
- **Take breaks without guilt.** Rest isn't falling behind. It's part of getting through.

## Let people help

People often want to help but don't know how. Specific requests are easier for them to say yes to:

- "Could you make the calls to the utility companies?"
- "Could you drive me to the bank on Tuesday?"
- "Could you sit with me while I go through the mail?"
- "Could you take the kids for the afternoon?"

If family members are sharing the work, a Tendwell plan lets everyone see what's being handled and by
whom.

## When grief and family meet

Grief can bring families closer, and it can also bring old tensions to the surface. People grieve
differently and at different speeds. One person may want to sort everything quickly; another may not
be able to face it yet.

It can help to:

- talk openly about who will do what
- be patient with different styles of grieving
- take a break from difficult conversations when emotions run high
- remember that most disagreements come from love and loss, not bad intentions

## Children and grief

Children grieve too, often in bursts between play. Simple, honest words help. It's okay to tell them
you're sad. Keeping familiar routines, like school, bedtime and meals, can give them a sense of
safety. Schools and pediatricians can often point you to support.

## Finding support

You don't have to manage this alone. Support comes in many forms:

- **Friends and family** who are good listeners
- **Hospice bereavement programs,** which usually support families for about a year, and often
  welcome people from the wider community
- **Support groups,** in person or online, including groups for specific losses
- **Grief counselors and therapists**
- **Faith communities**
- **Employee assistance programs** through work, which often offer free counseling

It's worth reaching out for extra help if, after some time, you feel unable to function day to day,
can't stop blaming yourself, or feel that life has no meaning.

## If you're struggling to stay safe

If you are thinking about harming yourself, or if grief feels unbearable, please reach out now. In the
US, you can **call or text 988** to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time of day or night.
It's free and confidential. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

## Be kind to yourself

You are doing something very hard. It's okay to make mistakes, to forget things, to miss a deadline
and to sort it out later. It's okay to laugh. It's okay to feel joy and grief on the same day.

Go gently. One small thing at a time is enough.
