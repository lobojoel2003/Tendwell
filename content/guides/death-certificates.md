A death certificate is the official record of a death. It's issued by the state where the death
happened, and it's the document almost every organization will ask to see before they will close an
account, pay a benefit or transfer property.

## How it's created

The death certificate is usually completed in two parts:

- **The medical part** is filled in by the doctor, hospice, medical examiner or coroner. It records
  the date, time and cause of death.
- **The personal part** is filled in by the funeral home, using details you give them, such as full
  name, date and place of birth, Social Security number, marital status, parents' names and
  occupation.

The funeral home then usually files the certificate with the state or county vital records office.

## How to get copies

Most families order copies through the funeral home. They can usually add the cost to their bill.

If you're not using a funeral home, or you need more copies later, contact the vital records office
in the state or county where the death happened. Many offer online ordering. Rules about who can
order a certified copy vary by state; close family members and the executor can usually do so.

## Certified copies and plain copies

A **certified copy** is printed on special paper with a raised or colored seal. Banks, insurers,
government agencies and courts usually want a certified copy.

A **photocopy or scan** is often enough for smaller organizations, such as utilities, phone
companies, subscriptions and some credit card companies. It's always worth asking: "Do you need a
certified copy, or will a photocopy do?"

## How many you might need

There's no perfect number. Many families find that **6 to 10 certified copies** is plenty. You may
need more if they had:

- several bank or investment accounts
- more than one life insurance policy
- property, such as a home or land
- pensions or retirement accounts
- vehicles

Extra copies ordered at the same time are often cheaper than the first. You can always order more
later, though it can take a few weeks.

## Who usually asks for one

You may be asked for a death certificate by:

- banks and credit unions
- life insurance companies
- employers and pension providers
- the probate court
- Social Security and other government agencies, if they need it
- the motor vehicle agency, if there's a car to transfer
- mortgage lenders and home insurers
- investment and retirement account providers
- the credit bureaus

## If it's delayed

Sometimes a death certificate takes longer than expected, often because the cause of death is still
being confirmed. In that case, the certificate may list the cause as "pending".

A pending certificate can still be useful. Many organizations will accept it to get started. Others
will wait for the final version. Ask the funeral home or the medical examiner's office how long it's
likely to take.

## If there's a mistake

Mistakes happen, often a misspelled name or a wrong date. It's worth checking the first copy you
receive carefully. Corrections are much easier to make early.

To correct a certificate, contact the funeral home or the state vital records office. They will
explain what proof they need, such as a birth certificate for a misspelled name.

## Keeping track of copies

It helps to keep a simple list of who you have sent a copy to, and whether they've returned it. You
can use the notes in your Tendwell plan.

Keep at least one certified copy at home, somewhere safe. You may need one months from now when
something unexpected turns up.

It's okay if this feels like a lot of paper. Each copy you send is one more thing taken care of.
