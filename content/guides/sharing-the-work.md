After a death, the practical work often falls to one person. That person can end up exhausted, while
others feel shut out or unsure how to help. Sharing the work, even a little, can make a real
difference for everyone.

## Start with a conversation

When you're ready, it helps to talk together, in person, by phone or on a video call. You don't need
to decide everything. A first conversation might cover:

- who is the executor, or who is likely to be
- what each person feels able to take on right now
- how you'll keep each other updated
- anything that needs a group decision, like the funeral

It's okay if not everyone can join, or if some people can only do a little.

## The executor still leads

If there is an executor, they have the legal responsibility for the estate. Others can help with many
tasks, but some decisions, like paying debts, selling property or sharing out the estate, belong to
the executor.

That doesn't mean they have to do everything. An executor can ask family to help with calls, letters,
research and sorting, while keeping the final decisions.

## Ways to divide the tasks

There's no single right way. Some families divide by:

- **Strengths.** Someone organized might handle paperwork; someone good on the phone might make
  calls.
- **Location.** Whoever lives nearest might look after the home, the mail and the car.
- **Time.** Someone with more free time might take on longer tasks, while someone busier takes quick
  ones.
- **Type of task.** One person takes banks and insurance, another takes subscriptions and online
  accounts.

In a Tendwell plan, anyone can say "I'll take this" on a step. Everyone can see who is handling what,
so nothing is done twice and nothing is forgotten.

## Keep each other in the loop

Many family disagreements after a death come from not knowing what's happening, rather than real
conflict. A few simple habits help:

- **Write down what you've done,** and who you spoke to.
- **Share updates regularly,** even short ones like "Called the bank, waiting for a letter."
- **Keep money matters transparent.** If you're the executor, share the estate's accounts with the
  beneficiaries.
- **Keep receipts** for anything you pay for, so it can be repaid fairly.

## Belongings and keepsakes

Dividing personal belongings can be harder than dividing money. Items carry memories, and two people
may want the same thing for different reasons.

Some approaches that help:

- **Check the will first.** It may say who receives specific items.
- **Take turns choosing,** one item each, in an order agreed in advance.
- **Make a list** of items people would like, and talk through any overlaps calmly.
- **Take photos** of things that can't be shared, so everyone has the memory.
- **Wait if you need to.** It's okay to leave belongings until emotions have settled.

## When things get tense

Grief can bring old family patterns to the surface. If conversations become difficult:

- take a break and come back to it another day
- focus on what the person who died would have wanted
- remember that people grieve differently and at different speeds
- put agreements in writing, so there are fewer misunderstandings
- consider a neutral person, like a family friend, a mediator or the estate's lawyer

If there's a serious disagreement about the will or the estate, it's worth getting legal advice
early.

## Helping from far away

If you live far away, there's still plenty you can do:

- make phone calls and send letters
- cancel subscriptions and online accounts
- research benefits and options
- help with forms and tax paperwork
- keep the shared list of accounts up to date
- check in on the person doing the most

## Look after the person doing the most

The executor or main helper often carries the heaviest load. A message, a meal or an offer to take
something off their list can mean a great deal. And if you are that person, it's okay to ask for help
directly.

Sharing the work doesn't have to be perfectly even. It only has to be kind.
