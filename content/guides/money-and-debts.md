Money worries are common after a death, especially if the person who died handled the household
finances. This guide explains what usually happens. It's general information, not financial or
legal advice, and rules vary by state.

## The most important thing to know

**In most cases, you are not personally responsible for a loved one's debts.** Debts are paid from
the estate, which is the money and property they left. If the estate doesn't have enough, some debts
may simply go unpaid.

You may be responsible if:

- you **co-signed** a loan or credit card
- you held a **joint account** or joint card with them
- you live in a **community property state** and were their spouse, for certain debts
- you're the executor and you didn't follow the state's rules for paying debts

If you're not sure, it's worth asking a lawyer before paying anything from your own money.

## Bank accounts

What happens to a bank account depends on how it was set up:

- **Joint accounts** usually pass straight to the other account holder.
- **Payable-on-death (POD) accounts** go to the named beneficiary.
- **Accounts in their name only** become part of the estate. The bank will usually freeze them until
  the executor can show the court's paperwork.

Try not to use money from an account in their name alone unless you're the executor, or the bank
says it's allowed. Even well-meant withdrawals can cause problems later.

## Bills that keep coming

Some bills need to keep being paid for a while, to protect the estate:

- mortgage or rent
- home insurance
- utilities for an empty home
- car insurance
- property taxes

Others can usually stop once you tell the company: subscriptions, memberships and phone plans, for
example.

The executor usually pays ongoing bills from an **estate bank account**. If a family member pays a
bill to protect the estate, such as the funeral or the home insurance, keep the receipt. They can
often be repaid from the estate.

## Funeral costs

Funeral costs are usually one of the first debts paid from the estate. If you pay for the funeral
yourself, keep every receipt. You may be able to be repaid once the estate is settled.

Life insurance, a prepaid funeral plan, a payable-on-death account or benefits for veterans may help
with costs. The Social Security lump-sum payment is small, but it can help too.

## Dealing with creditors

Creditors are the people and companies the person owed money to.

1. **Make a list** of credit cards, loans, medical bills and other debts.
2. **Tell each one about the death,** and give them the executor's name and address.
3. **Ask for a statement** of the balance at the date of death.
4. **Don't agree to pay anything over the phone.** Ask them to write to the executor.

The executor will pay valid debts from the estate in the order state law sets out. Funeral costs,
estate expenses and taxes usually come first.

## Debt collectors

A debt collector may call you, even if you don't owe anything. You have rights:

- A collector can talk to the executor or administrator about the debt.
- A collector shouldn't tell you or suggest that you have to pay from your own money if you aren't
  responsible.
- You can ask a collector, in writing, to stop contacting you.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) has plain-language information about debts and debt
collectors after a death.

## Some debts are cancelled

- **Federal student loans** are usually discharged when the borrower dies. Parent PLUS loans are
  discharged if the parent or the student dies.
- **Some private student loans** are also forgiven at death, depending on the lender.
- **Credit life insurance** may pay off a specific loan, like a car loan or mortgage.

## Protecting against fraud

Unfortunately, people sometimes try to use the identity of someone who has died. Telling the three
credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) about the death, and asking for a copy of their
credit report, helps protect against this. The report can also show debts you didn't know about.

## Taxes

The executor or a surviving spouse usually needs to file a final income tax return for the year of
death. Most estates don't owe federal estate tax, which only applies to very large estates, but a
few states have their own estate or inheritance taxes. An accountant can help.

## If money is tight

If the person who died was the main earner, things can feel frightening. Some places to look:

- life insurance, including through their work
- Social Security survivors benefits for a spouse or children
- pensions with survivor benefits
- final pay, unused vacation pay or other money owed by their employer
- veterans benefits, if they served

It's okay to ask for help. A nonprofit credit counselor can help you make a plan, often for free.
