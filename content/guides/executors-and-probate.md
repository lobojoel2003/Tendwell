The words _executor_, _estate_ and _probate_ can sound intimidating. In practice, settling an estate
is mostly a long list of ordinary tasks: finding papers, making calls, paying bills and keeping good
records. This guide explains the basics. Rules vary by state, so treat it as a starting point, not
legal advice.

## A few words you'll hear

- **Estate:** everything a person owned and owed when they died.
- **Will:** the document that says who they wanted to receive their property, and who should handle
  their affairs.
- **Executor:** the person named in the will to handle the estate. Some states use the term
  _personal representative_.
- **Administrator:** the person appointed by a court to handle the estate when there's no will, or
  no executor is able to act.
- **Probate:** the court process that confirms the will, appoints the executor and oversees the
  estate.
- **Beneficiary** or **heir:** someone who receives something from the estate.

## What an executor does

An executor's job is to look after the estate carefully and fairly until everything is settled.
That usually means:

1. finding the will and applying to the probate court, if needed
2. making a list of what the person owned and owed
3. keeping property safe and insured
4. telling banks, creditors and government agencies about the death
5. opening a bank account for the estate
6. paying debts, bills and taxes from the estate
7. filing final tax returns
8. sharing out what remains, as the will or state law says
9. keeping records and closing the estate

Executors have a legal duty to act in the best interests of the estate and its beneficiaries. They
can usually be reimbursed from the estate for reasonable expenses, and in many states they can also
be paid a fee.

## Being an executor is a choice

If you're named as executor, you can usually say no, or step down. If it feels like too much, the
will may name a backup, or the court can appoint someone else. An executor can also hire a lawyer or
accountant to do much of the work, paid from the estate.

## What probate involves

Probate looks different from state to state, and from estate to estate. In general:

1. **The executor files the will** and an application with the probate court in the county where the
   person lived.
2. **The court confirms the executor** and issues a document that proves their authority. This is
   often called _letters testamentary_, or _letters of administration_ if there's no will.
3. **The executor notifies creditors,** sometimes by publishing a notice in a local newspaper. There
   is usually a set period for creditors to make claims.
4. **The executor deals with the estate:** collecting money, paying debts and filing taxes.
5. **The executor distributes what's left** and, in many cases, files a final account with the
   court.

Simple estates can be settled in several months. Estates with property, disagreements or taxes can
take a year or more.

## Not everything goes through probate

Many things pass directly to someone without the court being involved:

- **jointly owned property,** such as a home owned "with right of survivorship"
- **joint bank accounts**
- **life insurance** with a named beneficiary
- **retirement accounts,** such as a 401(k) or IRA, with a named beneficiary
- **payable-on-death** and **transfer-on-death** accounts
- **property held in a living trust**

If most of what they owned passes this way, probate may be simple, or not needed at all.

## Small estates

Many states have a shorter, simpler process for smaller estates. It may be called a _small estate
affidavit_, _summary administration_ or something similar. The value limit and the rules vary a lot
between states. The probate court clerk can tell you whether it might apply.

## If there's no will

When someone dies without a will, they are said to have died _intestate_. State law then decides
who inherits, usually starting with a spouse and children, then parents, brothers and sisters, and
more distant relatives.

The court appoints an administrator, often a spouse or adult child, to do the same job as an
executor.

## When to ask a lawyer

Many estates can be handled without a lawyer. It's worth getting advice if:

- the estate includes a business, farmland or property in more than one state
- family members disagree, or someone may challenge the will
- the estate may not have enough to pay its debts
- there may be estate or inheritance tax
- you're not sure whether you're personally responsible for something
- you simply feel overwhelmed

Many probate lawyers offer a free first conversation, and their fees can usually be paid from the
estate. Your state or local bar association can help you find one.

## Looking after yourself as executor

Being an executor while grieving is a lot to carry. It's okay to go slowly. It's okay to ask family
to help with specific tasks. And it's okay to say, "I don't know yet, I'll find out." Clear records
and honest communication will protect you more than speed ever will.
