Telling people about a death is hard, and there can be a surprising number of people and places to
tell. You don't have to do it all at once, and you don't have to do it alone.

This guide sorts them roughly by how soon they need to know.

## In the first few days

These are the people who matter most right now.

- **Close family and friends.** A short phone call or message is enough.
- **Their employer,** if they were working.
- **Anyone they cared for or who cared for them,** such as a babysitter, home care agency or day
  program.
- **Their doctor,** if they died at home, so their records can be updated.

You can ask a trusted friend or relative to share the news with wider circles: neighbors, old
friends, colleagues and community groups.

## In the first few weeks

These organizations need to know so they can stop payments, protect accounts and tell you what
happens next.

- **Social Security.** The funeral home usually reports the death for you, but it's worth checking.
- **Banks and credit unions.**
- **Life insurance companies.**
- **Pension providers** and any other benefits they received.
- **The VA,** if they served in the military.
- **The three credit bureaus:** Equifax, Experian and TransUnion.
- **Their health insurer,** especially if family members are covered by the same plan.
- **The post office,** if their mail needs to be forwarded.

## In the first few months

These can usually wait until the executor has been appointed, or until you feel ready.

- **Credit card companies and lenders.**
- **Utilities, internet and phone providers.**
- **Their landlord,** if they rented.
- **Their home and car insurers.**
- **The mortgage lender.**
- **Investment and retirement account providers.**
- **The motor vehicle agency,** for their license and any vehicles.
- **The State Department,** to cancel a passport.
- **Online accounts,** such as email and social media.
- **Subscriptions and memberships.**
- **Their accountant, lawyer and financial adviser,** if they had them.

## Later on

- **The IRS and state tax agency,** when their final tax return is filed.
- **Professional licensing boards,** if they held a license.
- **Clubs, charities and alumni associations,** so mailings stop.

## Ways to make it easier

**Use one message for many people.** For friends and acquaintances, a single email or post can share
the news and the funeral details together.

**Keep a script nearby.** When calling organizations, it helps to have the basics written down:
their full name, date of birth, date of death, address, account number if you have it, and your
relationship to them.

**Ask for a bereavement team.** Many large companies have a team that only helps families after a
death. They are often kinder and quicker.

**Ask them to write to you.** You don't need to remember everything said on the phone. Ask for
confirmation in writing.

**Use a letter.** Tendwell has letter templates you can fill in and download as a PDF. Sending a
letter can be easier than a phone call, and it leaves a record.

**Share the list.** If family members are helping, divide the organizations between you. In a
Tendwell plan, everyone can see who is contacting whom, so no one gets called twice.

## What to have ready

Most organizations will ask for some of these:

- their full name and any previous names
- date of birth and date of death
- their address
- their Social Security number
- an account or policy number
- a copy of the death certificate
- your name, contact details and relationship to them
- if you are the executor, the court's paperwork that confirms it

It's okay not to have every detail. Most organizations can find an account with a name, date of
birth and address.
